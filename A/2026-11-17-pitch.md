I think the strongest direction is to frame the game around **how one insignificant moment becomes an ongoing form of collective humiliation — not because of one particularly cruel person, but because everyone keeps finding it funny.**

The important shift is that the game isn't necessarily about increasingly extreme forms of cyberbullying. It's about **the escalation of one single joke**, how it is repeated, transformed, taken further and further away from its original context, and how the group's reactions give it the power to continue.

Here is how I would articulate the concept.

## **1. The core concept**

# When a Joke Doesn't End

*An interactive game about cyberbullying, group dynamics, and the amplification of humiliation online.*

It starts with something insignificant.

A student does or says something slightly awkward at school. Someone captures the moment, takes it out of context, and turns it into a meme.

People laugh. They share it. The post gets attention.

So the joke continues.

The image becomes a sticker in group chats. The sticker becomes an AI-generated video. Students start reenacting the moment at school. Each new version builds on the previous one, reaching more people and becoming increasingly detached from what actually happened.

What began as a single joke becomes something the targeted student cannot escape.

**The game explores how one seemingly harmless incident can escalate into cyberbullying through repetition, collective participation, and social media amplification.**

The central mechanism is simple: **the joke survives because people keep laughing.**

Each like, share, comment, or recreation gives the joke another reason to exist. And while no single participant may feel responsible for what is happening, together they sustain the humiliation of one person.

Through this progression, players experience how the same situation can feel harmless to those watching and deeply harmful to the person being targeted.

The game invites players to question their own role in this dynamic: When does a joke become harassment? What does it mean to participate, even passively? And what happens when someone decides that it isn't funny anymore?

## **4. What the game is actually trying to make players understand**

I would distinguish three important layers.

**Repetition changes the meaning of a joke.**

Something that seems funny once can become deeply humiliating when it is repeated every day, by more and more people, in increasingly different contexts.

**The group gives harassment its power.**

The joke doesn't spread on its own. It continues because people laugh, share, imitate, and reward it with attention. Even small actions contribute to a larger dynamic.

**The experience is radically different depending on where you stand.**

For the group, it's a funny reference. For the targeted student, it can become something they are constantly confronted with, unable to control or escape.

There's also a fourth idea I find particularly important from your notes: **the first person who says "this isn't funny" is challenging the group, not just an individual bully.** That person may initially face ridicule or exclusion, but their reaction can also legitimize others who felt uncomfortable and were afraid to say anything.

That is a compelling potential game mechanic.

## **5. What is still open in the design**

You don't need to settle all the game mechanics to define the concept. I would keep three things open for now:

- **The exact incident:** Ideally chosen through conversations or workshops with teenagers, so it is genuinely relatable and doesn't require explaining why it became a meme.
- **The degree of interactivity:** The game could follow a linear escalation, or the player's reactions could influence whether the joke grows, changes, or loses momentum.
- **The platform and point of view:** It could unfold through a fictional social media feed, a school meme account, or another digital interface. The account's exact nature is secondary to the collective amplification mechanism.

## **6. Short pitch — for presentations**

**One joke. Seven posts. A whole school laughing.**

What happens when an embarrassing moment at school becomes a meme — and the joke never stops?

Our game follows a single, seemingly harmless incident as it is taken out of context, shared, repeated, and transformed across social media. What starts as a funny post gradually becomes a running joke that follows one student everywhere.

Through this escalation, the game explores how cyberbullying can emerge not from one deliberately cruel action, but from the accumulation of small acts of participation.

**Because a joke doesn't keep spreading on its own. It needs people to keep it alive.**

The game challenges players to recognize the power of the group, question their own participation, and understand when collective laughter becomes collective humiliation.

## **7. The one sentence I would use to anchor the whole project**

> **A game about how one harmless-looking joke becomes harassment when an entire group keeps it alive.**

I think the most important thing to preserve from your latest discussion is this distinction: **you're not telling a story about seven acts of bullying. You're telling a story about one act that never gets to end.**

That is what gives the project its coherence, and it also gives you a clear criterion for every design decision: *Does this new post show how the same joke gains power through repetition, transformation, or collective participation?*
